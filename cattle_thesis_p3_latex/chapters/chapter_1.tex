\section{Background}

Cattle monitoring involves assessing an animal's condition, recognizing its activities, and tracking changes over time. A cow may spend less time feeding, adopt a different resting posture, or gradually lose body condition. Interpreting these changes requires observations to be associated with the correct animal. Animal welfare research therefore emphasizes measures of the animal itself alongside information about its housing and management~\cite{weary2009understanding}.

Body Condition Scoring (BCS), behavior recognition, and individual identification provide complementary information for this purpose. BCS assesses body reserves through the appearance and tissue coverage of anatomical regions. Established scoring systems examine the pelvis, loin, spine, and tailhead rather than treating body size alone as an indicator of condition~\cite{edmonson1989bcs,ferguson1994bcs}. Behavior recognition identifies activities such as feeding, drinking, standing, lying, and walking. Individual identification links repeated observations to the same cow. Together, these tasks can provide a more informative record than any one task alone.

Collecting this information through manual observation requires time and experience, and brief inspections can miss short activities. Precision livestock farming uses sensing and analytical technologies to make monitoring more continuous and systematic. Sani et al.~\cite{sani2026ab25} and Lee et al.~\cite{lee2026ab26} describe the challenge of turning measurements into information that supports farm decisions. Reliable observations and continuity at the individual-animal level are important to that process.

Computer vision offers a common sensing approach for observing appearance, posture, and movement without attaching a sensor to every animal. Reviews of vision-based livestock monitoring describe applications across dairy management and wider production settings, while identifying difficulties associated with changing environments and use outside controlled conditions~\cite{antognoli2025ani1,zin2026ab26}. The value of vision is therefore not limited to automating isolated observations. It also offers a way to obtain several related measurements from images and video.

Deep learning enables visual features to be learned from data. Reusable encoders such as ResNet and EfficientNet reduce the need to design a separate feature-extraction method for each application~\cite{he2016resnet,efficientnet2019}. Cattle studies have used learned representations for condition assessment, activity recognition, and biometric matching. Integrating these capabilities, however, requires a representation that preserves the information each task needs.

Multi-task learning (MTL) trains several prediction tasks using shared model components and task-specific outputs. Sharing can help tasks learn useful common features, but it can also introduce competition for model capacity or favor features that are unsuitable for another task. Research on task grouping and selective sharing therefore treats the relationship between tasks as an empirical question~\cite{standley2020tasks,misra2016crossstitch}. This thesis investigates that relationship for cattle body condition, behavior, and identity.

\section{Rationale of the Study and Motivation}

\subsection{Task-Appropriate Visual Representations}

A common multi-task design attaches several prediction heads to a generic image encoder. Although this approach reuses much of the network, a shared input does not imply identical information requirements. Two tasks may examine the same cow while relying on different aspects of its appearance. The motivation for this study is to account for those differences when designing a unified monitoring framework.

BCS relies on body form and the visibility of condition-related anatomical features. Behavior recognition uses posture, movement, and the immediate setting. Re-identification (Re-ID) depends on stable differences between individuals, including coat markings and morphology. A feature that is useful for one task may therefore distract another. Coat pattern can distinguish cows, but should not substitute for body-condition assessment. A feeding area can help explain an activity, but identifying a cow from its usual enclosure may fail when it moves elsewhere.

This concern is related to shortcut learning, in which a model exploits convenient correlations rather than the intended relationship. Geirhos et al.~\cite{geirhos2020shortcut} describe this problem in deep learning, while Xiao et al.~\cite{xiao2021background} demonstrate the influence of image backgrounds on recognition. Farm images contain both animal appearance and environmental information. Distinguishing their roles matters when evaluating performance across cameras, farms, or recording conditions.

In this thesis, \emph{cattle-centered representation learning} means organizing visual information around the target animal and the requirements of the prediction task. Localization identifies the animal; segmentation separates its visible body from the surroundings; anatomical or pose information describes structure; and video captures changes in posture and position. These cues are candidates for evaluation, not components assumed to benefit every task. The aim is to determine what should be shared, what should remain task-specific, and when surrounding context is useful.

\subsection{Preliminary Investigations and Their Limitations}

Preliminary experiments used a generic pretrained visual backbone with multiple prediction heads. Body condition and behavior performance were lower under that multi-task configuration than under their single-task references. These observations motivated a closer examination of task-specific information requirements rather than treating the addition of multiple outputs as sufficient integration.

Reviewing the data and evaluation procedures also highlighted the relationships between observations. Different images may capture the same passage, adjacent moments in a recording, or synchronized views of one event. A passage label or temporary tracking identifier does not necessarily identify a biological animal. These distinctions affect what a train--test comparison measures, so the study groups observations according to the identity and recording information actually available.

Additional model complexity does not by itself address these issues. A component may produce useful localization without improving a downstream prediction, or preserve posture while discarding appearance needed for identity matching. We therefore use single-task models as references for evaluating the information supplied to each task before examining its use in a shared framework.

\subsection{Rationale for Jointly Studying the Three Tasks}

The selected tasks are related in practice but differ in their visual objectives. Condition assessment emphasizes morphology, behavior recognition emphasizes activity, and Re-ID emphasizes individual appearance. Their practical relationship motivates integration; their differences make the extent of sharing a research question rather than an assumption.

Unlike outputs that describe closely related aspects of one phenomenon, such as disease category and severity, these tasks require different responses to change. Identity features should remain useful across activities, whereas behavior features must respond to changes in activity. Studying the tasks together allows us to examine where common features are useful and where specialized processing is needed.

Pretrained perception models make these comparisons possible without training every upstream component from scratch. Promptable segmentation and transferable animal pose estimation provide candidate foreground and structural information~\cite{kirillov2023sam,ye2024superanimal}. The contribution of this work lies in evaluating and integrating task-appropriate representations, not in the use of these tools alone.

\section{Problem Statement}

This thesis addresses how visual representations can be learned and shared for cattle body condition scoring, behavior recognition, and re-identification without losing the information required by each task. Generic RGB features provide a useful starting point, but may combine animal appearance with environmental correlations. Extensive parameter sharing introduces a further challenge: one representation must support objectives that can favor different features.

BCS requires condition-related morphology to be distinguished from appearance that merely identifies an animal or its recording environment. Behavior recognition requires posture and temporal information without allowing location alone to determine the activity label. Re-ID requires individual appearance to remain useful across changes in camera geometry, background, and time. A favorable result for one task cannot establish that the same representation is suitable for all three.

Anatomy-focused BCS methods, temporal behavior models, metric-learning approaches to identification, and selective MTL architectures address different parts of this problem. The present study investigates their relationship: how cattle-centered information affects each task, how it can be shared, and how the resulting representations perform under different recording conditions.

The central research question is:
\begin{quote}
\textit{Can cattle-centered visual representations improve the robustness of body condition scoring, behavior recognition, and cow re-identification, and support a multi-task framework that reduces negative transfer compared with generic shared RGB representations?}
\end{quote}
Answering this question requires task performance to be considered alongside the visual information supplied, the sharing strategy, and the evaluation conditions.

\section{Objectives}

The main objective is to develop and evaluate a multi-task deep learning framework for BCS, behavior recognition, and cow Re-ID using task-appropriate cattle-centered visual representations.

The first objective is to establish evaluation protocols that respect the relationships within the available data. These protocols use animal identities where reliable biological identifiers exist and recording, passage, or session groups where they do not. This provides a foundation for interpreting comparisons between models.

The second objective is to establish single-task RGB reference models for the three tasks. These models provide a common basis for measuring the effect of representation changes and for identifying positive or negative transfer after integration.

The third objective is to investigate cattle-centered visual information, including localization, foreground masks, anatomical or pose cues, and relevant viewpoint information. For behavior recognition, the investigation also considers temporal information. The aim is to determine which combinations provide useful task-level information while remaining computationally practical.

The fourth objective is to compare extensive sharing with a task-conditioned approach that allows selected features or processing pathways to remain private. The comparison examines whether specialization can retain useful common information without sacrificing the requirements of individual tasks.

The fifth objective is to examine robustness under changes in acquisition conditions and across compatible datasets. This connects the model comparisons to the wider goal of monitoring animals outside the exact environments represented during training.

These objectives are expressed through three research questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item How do cattle-centered representations affect BCS, behavior recognition, and Re-ID relative to their RGB single-task references?
    \item How can temporal information and task-specific visual cues be incorporated while preserving the different requirements of condition assessment, activity recognition, and identity matching?
    \item How does task-conditioned sharing compare with hard sharing in task performance, negative transfer, and robustness under domain change?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methodology in Brief}

The study uses a comparative experimental design. Data preparation establishes the meaning of labels and the grouping units used for evaluation. ScienceDB provides the primary BCS setting, CVB and Kaggle Beef provide the primary behavior-training sources, and SideViewCows2026 provides the primary Re-ID setting. Separate collections are used because the tasks require different observations and annotations.

Single-task RGB models establish reference performance. Their cattle-centered counterparts use localization and binary-mask guidance, with temporal aggregation for behavior recognition. Anatomical, pose, and viewpoint cues are investigated separately rather than assumed to transfer reliably between datasets. Upstream perception is assessed before its outputs are used in a downstream model.

The multi-task comparison is designed to evaluate hard sharing against task-specific processing, using the single-task models as references. BCS evaluation measures the magnitude and ordering of prediction errors, behavior evaluation uses class-sensitive recognition measures, and Re-ID evaluation measures retrieval performance. Evaluation across compatible external datasets remains a separate assessment of changes in environment or acquisition conditions; it is not assumed to be complete.

Chapter 4 describes the data partitions, model configurations, training procedures, and experimental controls. Chapter 5 reports the corresponding results and their interpretation. Together, these comparisons examine which information each task retains and how performance changes when learning is shared.

\section{Scopes and Challenges}

The evaluated framework covers BCS, behavior recognition, and individual cow identification or re-identification. Lameness detection is not included. RGB imagery provides the common visual basis; depth-derived and other external data are considered only where their role and label compatibility can be established. The framework is a research contribution to animal monitoring, not a replacement for veterinary assessment.

The tasks and datasets provide different forms of supervision. Body condition labels vary in range and convention. Behavior categories depend on posture and activity, and some categories occur only in particular sources. Re-ID requires reliable identity information and appropriate separation between learning and evaluation observations. ScienceDB supports burst-group-disjoint, sequence-safe evaluation rather than biological cow-disjoint evaluation. These distinctions limit the claims that can be drawn from each experiment.

Viewpoint, occlusion, scale, and lighting also affect the available visual evidence. Foreground processing can suppress irrelevant background while removing useful context. Pose estimation can describe body structure without recovering every anatomical feature needed for condition scoring. We therefore treat component selection as an experimental question.

Computational constraints motivate pretrained models, reusable preprocessing, and focused comparisons. Chapter 2 reviews the relevant literature and research gap. Chapter 3 sets out the requirements, impacts, and constraints. Chapters 4 and 5 present the methodology and experimental analysis, and Chapter 6 brings together the conclusions.

The single-task contribution comprises leakage-aware protocols, matched RGB and cattle-centered comparisons, and reporting of perception coverage and negative findings. The comparisons show task-dependent outcomes: ordered BCS error improved, behavior gains varied by class and source, and oracle-guided Re-ID gains were largest under snapshot shift. These findings concern the evaluated single-task configurations; they do not by themselves establish successful multi-task integration or negative-transfer mitigation.
