\section{Background}

Cattle monitoring requires us to understand an animal's condition, its behavior, and how both change over time. A cow may eat less, rest differently, or slowly lose body condition. To understand these changes properly, the observations also need to be linked to the correct animal. Animal welfare research therefore gives importance to measurements taken directly from the animal, together with information about its housing and management~\cite{weary2009understanding}.

Body Condition Scoring (BCS), behavior recognition, and individual identification provide three useful types of information. BCS estimates body reserves from the appearance and tissue coverage of important body regions. Common scoring systems look at areas such as the pelvis, loin, spine, and tailhead instead of using body size alone~\cite{edmonson1989bcs,ferguson1994bcs}. Behavior recognition identifies activities such as feeding, drinking, standing, lying, and walking. Individual identification links repeated observations to the same cow. Together, these tasks can give a more complete picture of an animal than any one task by itself.

Collecting this information manually takes time and experience, and short observations can easily miss brief activities. Precision livestock farming uses sensors and data analysis to make monitoring more regular and continuous. Sani et al.~\cite{sani2026ab25} and Lee et al.~\cite{lee2026ab26} discuss the challenge of turning collected measurements into useful information for farm decisions. For this to work, the observations need to be reliable and connected to the correct animal over time.

Computer vision provides a useful way to observe appearance, posture, and movement without attaching a separate sensor to every animal. Reviews of vision-based livestock monitoring describe many applications in dairy farming and other livestock settings, but they also report problems when models are used under changing or less controlled conditions~\cite{antognoli2025ani1,zin2026ab26}. Therefore, the value of computer vision is not only that it can automate one task. It can also provide several related measurements from the same image or video source.

Deep learning allows these visual features to be learned directly from data. Reusable image encoders such as ResNet and EfficientNet reduce the need to design a separate feature-extraction method for every task~\cite{he2016resnet,efficientnet2019}. Previous cattle studies have used learned features for body condition assessment, behavior recognition, and individual matching. However, combining these tasks is more difficult because each task may need different visual information.

Multi-task learning (MTL) trains several tasks with some shared model components and separate task outputs. Sharing can help tasks reuse useful features, but it can also hurt performance if the shared features are not suitable for every task. Research on task grouping and selective sharing therefore treats the relationship between tasks as something that should be tested rather than assumed~\cite{standley2020tasks,misra2016crossstitch}. This thesis studies that problem for cattle body condition, behavior, and identity.

\section{Rationale of the Study and Motivation}

\subsection{Task-Appropriate Visual Representations}

A common MTL design uses one image encoder and places several prediction heads after it. This is simple and efficient, but the fact that several tasks use the same image does not mean that they need the same visual information. Two tasks may look at the same cow while focusing on very different features. This study is motivated by the need to handle those differences inside one framework.

BCS mainly depends on body shape and visible condition-related anatomy. Behavior recognition depends on posture, movement, and sometimes the nearby environment. Re-identification (Re-ID) depends on stable differences between cows, such as coat markings and body shape. Because of this, a feature that helps one task may distract another. Coat pattern is useful for telling two cows apart, but it should not become a shortcut for predicting body condition. A feeding area may help explain an activity, but a Re-ID model should not recognize a cow only because it usually appears in one location.

This problem is related to shortcut learning. In shortcut learning, a model uses an easy correlation instead of learning the relationship that we actually want it to learn. Geirhos et al.~\cite{geirhos2020shortcut} describe this problem in deep learning, and Xiao et al.~\cite{xiao2021background} show that image backgrounds can strongly affect recognition. Farm images contain both the cow and its surroundings, so it is important to understand which information the model is using, especially when the camera, farm, or recording condition changes.

In this thesis, \emph{cattle-centered representation learning} means organizing the visual input around the target animal and the needs of the task. Localization finds the target cow. Segmentation separates the visible cow from the surrounding scene. Pose or anatomical information can describe body structure, while video can show changes in posture and movement. These cues are treated as options to test, not as features that are automatically assumed to help every task. The goal is to find what information can be shared, what should stay task-specific, and when surrounding context is still useful.

\subsection{Preliminary Investigations and Their Limitations}

Preliminary experiments used a generic pretrained visual backbone with several prediction heads. In that earlier multi-task setup, body condition and behavior performed worse than their single-task references. This showed that simply adding several outputs to one shared network was not enough. It motivated us to study the information needed by each task more carefully.

The data review also showed that different images are not always independent. Several images may come from the same cow passage, nearby moments in the same video, or synchronized views of one event. A passage label or a temporary tracking ID is also not necessarily a true biological cow ID. These details affect what a train--test split actually measures. Therefore, the study groups observations using the identity and recording information that is really available in each dataset.

Adding more model components also does not automatically solve these problems. A component may improve localization but still fail to improve the final task. A representation may preserve posture but remove appearance that is useful for identity matching. For this reason, we use single-task models as reference points before studying how the tasks should be combined in one framework.

\subsection{Rationale for Jointly Studying the Three Tasks}

The three tasks are related in practice, but they need different visual information. BCS focuses on morphology, behavior recognition focuses on activity, and Re-ID focuses on individual appearance. Their connection makes integration useful, while their differences make the amount of sharing an important research question.

These tasks also react differently to change. Identity features should remain useful even when the cow changes activity, while behavior features must change when the activity changes. This is different from combining two very similar outputs, such as disease type and disease severity. Studying BCS, behavior, and Re-ID together allows us to examine where shared features help and where task-specific processing is needed.

Pretrained perception models make these comparisons possible without training every supporting model from the beginning. Promptable segmentation and transferable animal pose estimation can provide foreground and structural information~\cite{kirillov2023sam,ye2024superanimal}. However, simply using these tools is not the contribution of the thesis. The contribution comes from testing how task-appropriate cattle-centered information affects the three tasks and how that information can be combined.

\section{Problem Statement}

This thesis studies how visual representations can be learned and shared for cattle BCS, behavior recognition, and Re-ID without losing the information needed by each task. Generic RGB features are a useful starting point, but they may mix useful animal information with background or environmental correlations. Heavy parameter sharing creates another problem because one shared representation must support tasks that may prefer different features.

For BCS, the model should focus on body-condition information instead of learning features that only identify the cow or its environment. For behavior recognition, the model needs posture and temporal information without simply learning that one activity usually happens in one location. For Re-ID, the model must preserve individual appearance even when camera angle, background, or time changes. A good result on one task is therefore not enough to show that the same representation is suitable for all three tasks.

Previous work on anatomy-based BCS, temporal behavior recognition, metric-learning Re-ID, and selective MTL provides different parts of the solution. This study brings these ideas together and asks how cattle-centered information changes each task, how much of that information should be shared, and how the models behave under different recording conditions.

The central research question is:
\begin{quote}
\textit{Can cattle-centered visual representations improve the robustness of body condition scoring, behavior recognition, and cow re-identification, and support a multi-task framework that reduces negative transfer compared with generic shared RGB representations?}
\end{quote}
To answer this question, we need to consider not only task performance, but also the visual information given to the model, the sharing strategy, and the conditions used for evaluation.

\section{Objectives}

The main objective is to develop and evaluate a multi-task deep learning framework for BCS, behavior recognition, and cow Re-ID using task-appropriate cattle-centered visual representations.

The first objective is to create evaluation protocols that respect the relationships inside each dataset. Biological cow identities are used where reliable IDs exist. Recording, passage, or session groups are used where they do not. This gives a safer basis for comparing models.

The second objective is to establish single-task RGB reference models for the three tasks. These models provide the baseline needed to measure the effect of representation changes and to identify positive or negative transfer after integration.

The third objective is to study cattle-centered visual information, including localization, foreground masks, anatomical or pose cues, and viewpoint information. For behavior recognition, temporal information is also considered. The goal is to find which combinations are useful for each task while keeping the system practical to train and evaluate.

The fourth objective is to evaluate alternative strategies for integrating the three tasks, including hard parameter sharing, task-private residual adaptation, and conflict-aware shared-gradient optimization, using matched single-task models as references for negative-transfer analysis.

The fifth objective is to examine robustness under the source and acquisition changes available within the primary evaluation protocols and to define compatible external datasets for future stress testing.

These objectives are expressed through three research questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item How do cattle-centered representations affect BCS, behavior recognition, and Re-ID relative to their RGB single-task references?
    \item How can temporal information and task-specific visual cues be incorporated while preserving the different requirements of condition assessment, activity recognition, and identity matching?
    \item How does task-conditioned sharing compare with hard sharing in task performance, negative transfer, and robustness under domain change?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methodology in Brief}

The study uses a comparative experimental design. The data preparation stage first defines the labels and the grouping units used for evaluation. ScienceDB is the primary BCS dataset, CVB and Kaggle Beef are the main behavior sources, and SideViewCows2026 is the main Re-ID dataset. Different datasets are used because the three tasks need different types of observations and annotations.

Single-task RGB models are used as reference models. Their cattle-centered versions use localization and binary-mask guidance, while the behavior model also uses temporal aggregation. Pose, anatomy, and viewpoint cues are studied separately instead of assuming that they will transfer well across datasets. Upstream perception is checked before its output is used in a downstream model.

The multi-task investigation evaluates alternative integration strategies against matched single-task cattle-centered reference models on identical held-out test sets. A monolithic hard-shared multi-task model evaluates full parameter sharing across tasks. A modular task-private multi-task model evaluates architectural specialization using dedicated residual bottleneck adapters. A gradient-projected multi-task optimization control (PCGrad) evaluates conflict-aware shared-gradient projection while keeping the hard-shared architecture and parameter count strictly matched. Task-wise held-out comparisons quantify outcome-level negative transfer across prediction error for BCS, class-sensitive metrics for behavior recognition, and open-set retrieval for cow re-identification. Compatible external datasets provide candidate protocols for future cross-environment stress testing, treated separately from primary benchmark evaluations.

Chapter 4 describes the data splits, model configurations, training procedures, and experimental controls. Chapter 5 presents the results and their interpretation. Together, these experiments examine what information each task needs and what changes when learning is shared.

\section{Scopes and Challenges}

The framework covers three visual tasks: BCS, behavior recognition, and individual cow identification or Re-ID. Lameness detection is not part of the thesis scope. RGB imagery is the common visual input. Depth and other external data are considered only when their role and label compatibility are clear. The framework is intended as a research contribution to cattle monitoring, not as a replacement for veterinary assessment.

The tasks and datasets do not provide the same type of supervision. BCS labels differ in range and scoring convention. Behavior classes depend on posture and activity, and some classes appear only in certain data sources. Re-ID needs reliable cow identity information and a clear separation between learning and evaluation data. ScienceDB supports burst-group-disjoint, sequence-safe evaluation, not biological cow-disjoint evaluation. These differences limit the claims that can be made from each experiment.

Viewpoint, occlusion, scale, and lighting also affect what can be seen in an image. Foreground processing can remove irrelevant background, but it may also remove useful context. Pose estimation can describe body structure, but it may still miss anatomical information needed for BCS. Therefore, the study treats component selection as something that must be tested experimentally.

Computational limits also affect the design. We therefore use pretrained models, reusable preprocessing, and focused comparisons instead of making the architecture unnecessarily large. Chapter 2 reviews the related research and the research gap. Chapter 3 describes the requirements, impacts, and constraints. Chapters 4 and 5 present the methodology and experimental analysis, and Chapter 6 presents the conclusions.

The completed study combines leakage-aware evaluation, matched single-task cattle-centered comparisons, and controlled multi-task experiments. The single-task results show task-dependent benefits from cattle-centered representations, while the multi-task comparisons show outcome-level degradation under hard sharing and selective mitigation under alternative architectural and optimization strategies. These findings motivate a unified framework that preserves shared cattle information while recognizing task-specific representation and optimization requirements.
