\section{Preliminaries}

In the case of a unified cattle monitoring system, it is necessary to utilize various types of visual cues jointly. In particular, body condition relies mostly on body structure, whereas behavior relies on body posture and motion. On the contrary, identity is defined by a set of visual features, which allow distinguishing between individuals. That is why the thesis merges concepts from livestock monitoring, visual representation learning and multi-task learning.

This chapter will provide an overview of the existing studies of different types of visual cues and evaluation strategies for them. First of all, the basic terminology regarding the three tasks will be discussed. Then, cattle specific models, foreground processing, anatomy and pose, temporal information and task specific feature sharing will be analyzed. Finally, these concepts will be used to describe the research gap addressed in this thesis.

\subsection{Visual Representations and Transfer Learning}

The visual representation is the set of features that the model uses to represent an image or a video frame sequence. Previously developed recognition algorithms relied on manually selected features. Neural networks trained on a dataset learn relevant features without any design effort on the part of engineers. In the case of convolutional neural networks, local primitive features are composed to produce more sophisticated ones gradually. The visual representations fed to the predictor define the type of information the model uses to make predictions.

Reusable image feature extractors have enabled this step in specialized domains. ResNet~\cite{he2016resnet} uses residual links to facilitate the training of deep networks. EfficientNet~\cite{efficientnet2019} optimizes the scaling of network depth, width, and input size together. Even though they solve different problems in their designs, both architectures may be useful starting points for transfer learning. In this particular instance, instead of using the machine learning algorithms to learn all the visual features of the cattle based on the limited dataset available, the pretrained machine could be adapted for this particular use.

Transfer learning and cattle-centered representation learning are not the same in terms of functionality. Transfer learning focuses on the source of initial weights of the model. Cattle-centered learning gives us a clue as to what kind of input data was fed to the model. The same pre-trained encoder may be used with different types of input data such as full image, crop of the cow, masked cow, and anatomically detailed images. That is why the encoder will stay the same regardless of the changes in the input data. This difference is important when comparing why one representation performs better than another.

\subsection{Prediction Tasks and Their Visual Requirements}

There are different approaches for predicting BCS, which are classification, ordinal prediction, and regression. In classification, all the scores can be treated as individual classes. In ordinal prediction, order is significant since the prediction of a neighboring score is a less significant error compared to the one which is distant.  However, regression just tries to predict the numeric output. CORAL is one example of an algorithm designed for the ordered labels~\cite{coral2020}. This algorithm may teach the classifier about the order of BCS, but it will not choose the visual features of the cow.

In the case of behavior recognition, a class is actually an activity that is not measured by numbers or scores. One frame can be able to inform us about the posture of a cow , whereas multiple frames can show how the cow moves over time. It is important since there are activities that might be quite similar in one frame only, for example, standing and walking. which can look almost the same in a single frame. So, the model should use a suitable amount of time for the detection task.

Identification and Re-ID are two distinct tasks. Closed set classifier identifies the cows seen in the training dataset. On the other hand , the query image is compared with the gallery images based on the embedding learned in Re-ID. It becomes possible for the system to be tested on cows unseen during the training phase. The good Re-ID embedding will classify images of the same cow into one cluster and images of the other cows into different clusters. The significance of this technique in cattle research lies in the changing herd of cattle~\cite{andrew2021opencows}.

\subsection{Multi-Task Learning and Negative Transfer}

Multi-task learning entails the training of different tasks in the same model where particular parts of the model are common and the training objective is minimizing the total loss of all the tasks,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MTL}}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_t\mathcal{L}_t,
\end{equation}
where in this equation, $\mathcal{L}_t$ is the loss of task $t$, and $\lambda_t$ determines the weight of the loss of task $t$. This equation calculates the total loss; however, but there is no information about what amount of the network should be shared. A neural network model can share all layers, a few layers, or exchange information between tasks.

In the survey paper by Crawshaw~\cite{crawshaw2020mtl}, MTL is considered in the context of information sharing, task-specific components, and optimization. MTL could be advantageous if one task is providing some useful information for another task. However, joint training could be harmful to a specific task; that is, the effect is referred to as negative transfer. It might occur due to the inappropriateness of information shared between tasks, conflicting task gradients, and unbalanced training losses. It is critical to distinguish information sharing and training for the purpose of the thesis.

\section{Review of Existing Research}

\subsection{From Automated Observations to Integrated Livestock Monitoring}

The development of the livestock monitoring technology consists of moving from individual measurements towards integrated systems of sensing, prediction, and decision-making. One can see this in the reviews that were written about computer vision as well as in the precision livestock literature in general. Within the scope of this particular question, the work of Antognoli et al.~\cite{antognoli2025ani1} has to do with computer vision in dairy farming, whereas the research by Zin and Tin~\cite{zin2026ab26} refers to computer vision in livestock in general. It shows that image recognition is not the only part of livestock monitoring.

First, the image should give a proper look at the animals themselves. Palma et al.~\cite{palma2025ani1} revealed that predictive analysis is applied more frequently than prescriptive decision support in the dairy literature they examined. Similarly, Sani et al.~\cite{sani2026ab25} pointed out that it is challenging to employ precision livestock technologies on-farm. This means that mere data collection or enhancing the performance of one classifier is not sufficient. The results need to be tracked, as well as associated with the right animal and farm condition.

Sensor-based and vision-based systems yield different types of information. As Lee et al.~\cite{lee2026ab26} indicated, biosensors enable continuous information acquisition about individual animals, whereas cameras make it possible to capture the appearance and behavior of the animals directly. The importance of vision for this thesis comes from the fact that information about the body shape, posture, movements, and coat patterns is available through images and videos. All the three tasks utilize the same type of information source, namely visual information, though they require different information out of it.

There is no need to have exactly the same components for all the tasks in the unified cattle monitoring system. Some components, like cameras, preprocessing, and shared features can be the same, but others will be different for each particular task. Thus, apart from integration of different tasks' predictions, the process includes identification of those features that should be used for all tasks and those that should stay specific.

\subsection{Body Condition Scoring: Anatomy, Image Geometry, and Ordered Prediction}

Automation of BCS is highly dependent on the cow's anatomy. Edmonson et al.~\cite{edmonson1989bcs} introduced the scoring index, whereas Ferguson et al.~\cite{ferguson1994bcs} explored the connection between the animal's anatomy and certain BCS values. Regarding the computer vision aspect, body mass of the cow alone is not enough as an indicator. Indeed, there should be considered such parameters as tissue covering, body shape, and crucial body parts. Hence, camera angles and the body parts themselves become very important.

Another approach uses depth images for better representation of the body shape of a cow. In particular, Rodriguez Alvarez et al.~\cite{rodriguez2018bcs} employ convolutional neural networks along with depth images and validate the prediction of BCS by means of various error thresholds. This approach relies on the combination of body geometry and features rather than the handcrafted features alone. The paper also proves that exact accuracy and tolerance-based accuracy have to be evaluated separately because of the fact that BCS scores are ordinal.

Another methodology makes use of ordinary RGB images to detect visual cues associated with body condition. In the case of Liu et al.~\cite{liu2025vets}, most of the feature extraction takes place in the tail area and is done using the EfficientNet-B0 architecture with channel and spatial attention. Their work involves the selection of a specific body part and an efficient network architecture, while distillation assists in making the model size smaller. The fact that BCS prediction can be tailored through a combination of the specific body part and architecture has been illustrated.

Camera view influences the information that can be obtained by the model as well. Yao et al.~\cite{yao2026jds2} select side-view pictures of the cattle using a ConvNeXt-based regression model and cow-level cross-validation. One method is focused on the tail of the animal; therefore, it observes just a small portion of the body, whereas another method selects side-view pictures and considers a bigger part of the cow's body. Both methods are different not only in their models but also in the parts of the cow they observe.

The research done on cattle measurement also reveals that there is a need for distinction between image size and true physical measurements. The authors Guzhva et al.~\cite{guzhva2026s415} came up with a system called PickAMoo, which uses smartphone images, LiDAR scaling, and Mask R-CNN segmentation in order to estimate weight. Even though the purpose of estimating the weight is different from estimating BCS, this method is relevant since the estimation is done when the animal has been separated from the image, and pixels are related to physical measurements.

The common factor among all these papers can be seen in three aspects that make good components for BCS prediction. First is the use of images that have proper features of the body and its orientation. Second is the use of an appropriate technique to extract such information, for example, cropping, body contour, depth map, or attention. Third is selecting an appropriate prediction technique that is suited to ordered BCS. CORAL can handle ordering of output classes~\cite{coral2020}, but it will not perform well if the input class does not have appropriate condition features. Thus, body-shape information will be considered as part of the representation, and ordinal prediction as the model choice.

\subsection{Behavior Recognition: Posture, Motion, and Environmental Context}

Behavior classes can differ in both appearance and duration. Lying may often be recognized from body posture, while walking is better described by movement. Feeding and drinking also involve interaction with a resource such as a trough. Because of this, behavior research uses different amounts of spatial and temporal information, and a single-frame classifier cannot describe every type of activity equally well.

Image-based behavior recognition shows that posture can already provide useful information. Asim et al.~\cite{asim2026s260} use overhead-camera images and object-detection models to recognize activities including standing, lying, grazing, and estrus-related behavior. Their method is vision-based rather than wearable-sensor based. It shows one way to connect animal localization and activity labels in a frame. A sequence model can then add information about how those visible postures change over time.

Video datasets extend the task beyond one frame. CBVD-5, introduced by Li et al.~\cite{li2024cbvd5}, contains standing, lying, foraging, rumination, and drinking. CVB, introduced by Zia et al.~\cite{zia2023cvb}, provides densely sampled behavior clips from an open-pasture setting and includes a SlowFast benchmark. The datasets have different environments and behavior labels, but both show that behavior recognition depends on the animal and on how the activity is defined and observed over time.

MmCows~\cite{vu2024mmcows} is the fusion of synchronized sensor data, information about the cow and video data. The annotations of MmCows include identity, location and behavior of the cows observed from different viewpoints. One can see whether behavioral predictions stay consistent across viewpoints and individual cows. Although a video dataset with tracking IDs is still useful in terms of motion study, it cannot provide the same level of biological identity information.

These demonstrate two different aspects of behavioral data: temporal information and identity consistency. Video in high density helps with motion analysis in short term. Identity of the cows helps with the test being done on different cows and across time. The one does not substitute for the other. This means that a study that requires both needs to factor in both and not just choose the one with many images.

Contextual background information can also be useful and detrimental. While a dip or feeding barrier could aid in learning about feeding/drinking, the problem is that it might learn that one task tends to happen in one camera quadrant. While the removal of the entire background will make the shortcut less obvious, it will also take out some interaction information. It is not about whether the context can be seen as positive or negative. What matters is the amount of context that is helpful to comprehend the activity and its ability to remain relevant despite changes in the environment.

This is linked to the cow-focused thesis in which the cow is still the key element of the visual reference and where posture, movement and context relationship can all be used if it is necessary. It also allows for the use of a class-based assessment – a model can perform well on common rest behaviours and poorly on rare or more dynamic ones. This can be obscured by the overall accuracy if class frequency is correlated with source.

\subsection{Individual Identification and Re-ID: From Coat Patterns to Cross-Setting Recognition}

The visual identification of cattle requires certain characteristics that differ from one animal to another. Andrew et al.~\cite{andrew2017cattle} use the technique of local coat pattern matching through RGB-D images. This technique provides us with an example of how the useful characteristic of identity is made explicit: local patterns are able to identify Holstein-Friesian cattle. This technique also indicates an important drawback that remains relevant for today's models. The identity-related feature must be visible in both images that are being compared.

Deep metric learning changes how these comparisons are learned. Andrew et al.~\cite{andrew2021opencows} use an embedding-based method with a combined classification and reciprocal-triplet objective. Instead of only using a classifier for known cows, the embedding supports similarity-based matching. This allows the feature space to be tested on cows that were not used to train the encoder. The goal therefore changes from memorizing fixed identity labels to learning features that can separate animals more generally.

Face-based identification uses a different body region. Weng et al.~\cite{weng2022cattleface} use a two-branch convolutional network to combine cattle-face information. Compared with coat-based methods, this focuses on facial differences between cows. Which approach is more useful depends on the camera view. A clear face may be hard to obtain from an overhead or rear view, while a useful coat pattern may be hidden by occlusion or missing from a tight face crop. Camera position and visible body coverage are therefore important parts of Re-ID.

Another line of work reduces the amount of manual identity labeling needed in video. Gao et al.~\cite{gao2021cows2021} use the Cows2021 dataset to study self-supervision from tracking-based observations and normalized cow views. Yu et al.~\cite{yu2025multicam} extend this idea to several cameras on a working farm with MultiCamCows2024. These methods use the continuity of video to connect images that are likely to show the same cow. A short tracklet can therefore help the model learn appearance consistency without requiring a manually assigned cow ID for every frame.

Nevertheless, tracking and Re-ID are still different tasks. While tracking maintains association of an animal when it stays continuously visible, re-identification must restore this association when there is a gap in time or change in viewing conditions. Information about tracking may be used for training of the Re-ID model, but an id of the temporary tracking does not correspond to biological ID of the cow. Multi-camera and cross-setting assessment would be helpful since it examines bigger shifts than a continuous video sequence.

The other problem is identification over a prolonged period of time. The BECA database~\cite{zhang2026beca} proposed by Zhang et al. contains not only a big cattle population but also a longitudinal setting. This setting differs from the short-term identification of dairy cows which can possess unique and distinctive patterns. The appearance of the animal can change due to the size, position, and body parts depicted in the image. The bigger the cattle population, the more similar the animals can become to each other. Therefore, a Re-ID system should not only be assessed in terms of the identification of trained cattle population but also in terms of others.

Similarly, foreground processing and anatomy processing have to take into account the need to preserve the data needed for identification purposes. While deleting the background may force the model to concentrate on the cow, deleting too much of it by reducing it to just a silhouette or skeleton may lead to loss of critical coat features. Retaining the full environment will cause the opposite effect by teaching the model the usual position of the cow. Consequently, Re-ID will require a selective representation technique that minimizes environmental shortcuts but maintains cow-specific characteristics.

\subsection{Localization and Segmentation as Representation Design}

The localization determines the cow on which the model needs to be applied. This becomes very crucial when there are multiple cows in the same image since the predicted class or behavior belongs to that particular cow only. The bounding box provides an effective ROI, but also maintains some context within the image. Image cropping can normalize the scales and enable the encoder to focus, but alters the body-background ratio.

There are several modern object detectors that can help to locate this region. RT-DETR developed by Zhao et al.~\cite{zhao2024rtdetr} uses an end-to-end transformer detector optimized for speed-accuracy trade-off. Instance segmentation approaches are richer than bounding box. Mask R-CNN extends the detection network with the mask branch and thus makes it predict not only the position but also object specific mask~\cite{he2017maskrcnn}. To be more precise, the bounding box says where the cow is, while the mask defines its pixels.

Promptable segmentation decouples the process of selecting the target from creating its mask. Promptable segmentation model, Segment Anything by Kirillov et al.~\cite{kirillov2023sam}, is capable of generating masks for prompts such as points and boxes. Promptable segmentation in SAM 2, being memory-based, is applicable to both images and video~\cite{ravi2024sam2}. This implies that in a cattle pipeline, for instance, we can first detect the cow, and then prompt the segmentation model to generate the mask, without the need to train a new cattle-specific segmentation model from scratch.  The final mask will depend upon how the subject was chosen and prompted.

Supervision becomes more straightforward in cattle-specific datasets. CattleEyeView~\cite{ong2023cattleeyeview} is a dataset with annotations from a top-view for detection, counting, tracking, pose estimation, and instance segmentation tasks. As multiple annotation types describe the same scenes, the data set is relevant to cattle perception. Nevertheless, these tasks of cattle perception are different from predicting BCS, Behavior, and Re-ID. They are mentioned in this thesis to characterize the cow, yet, they do not provide proof of the representation being suitable for all other tasks.

Recent cattle recognition works relate foreground detection and cattle identity learning. CATR-Net~\cite{feng2025catr} uses segmentation and cattle face recognition techniques in combination with edge-based segmentation and attention-based recognition. It associates the process of separating the cattle from the background with identity-related characteristics. In addition to previous approaches based on coat appearance and face recognition, this illustrates the relevance of the cattle-centered recognition as an active research area. The question remains open whether foreground-oriented processing is beneficial for tasks depending more on body shape or movement as well.

Segmentation and feature attention are also different. CBAM changes the importance of channel and spatial features inside a network~\cite{cbam2018}, while a foreground mask changes or weights the image content using an estimated object boundary. Attention can focus on useful regions while still keeping the full image. Masking can reduce background information before the main encoder. The two methods may work together, but they are not the same intervention.

Masking could also be applied with different degrees of strength. With hard foreground masking, all content outside of the estimated boundary is eliminated. The softer form of masking will lessen the effect of the background while retaining some context along with uncertain boundary information. It is a matter of selecting the appropriate approach for the particular task. Useful body contours are required for BCS, context is needed for behavior, and coat information for Re-ID. Therefore, the quality of a mask should be assessed together with the performance of the downstream task, rather than only by how clean the mask appears.

\subsection{Pose, Anatomy, and Viewpoint}

Pose estimation represents an animal using the positions of selected body parts. DeepLabCut shows that user-defined landmarks can be learned with transfer learning from a relatively small amount of annotation~\cite{mathis2018deeplabcut}. This changes the input from dense appearance information to a structured description of body configuration. Such information can support movement analysis and can also make some parts of the model input easier to understand.

This is precisely what SuperAnimal~\cite{ye2024superanimal} does by fusing various animal poses datasets to train models that will be applicable to different animals. SuperAnimal's training strategy is flexible enough to incorporate differences in landmark definition, and it can either be directly applied or further modified. This is beneficial for cattle studies as it makes it possible not to start building a new pose dataset from scratch. Another study, which gives a more specific example is CattleEyeView~\cite{ong2023cattleeyeview}, where a top-down approach was chosen specifically for cattle.

Overall, these studies provide an example of how structural information can be obtained in two ways -- through transfer learning using different animal datasets and through direct training based on a cattle-specific dataset. Also, the definition of landmarks plays an important role. The skeletal information can be gathered from the head, the body, and legs, but BCS requires more. Thus, the BCS features that were investigated by Ferguson et al.~\cite{ferguson1994bcs} include much more than just positions of joints. Pose and body configuration are related, but these are different representations of the data. The BCS may require both the structural coordinates and visual features while Behavior will probably rely on the changes in the coordinates in time.

Viewpoint impacts the visibility of body parts and appearance of the cow in the image. The rear, side, and top views may represent different body configurations despite the fact that the condition of the cow remains the same. This point is important for the task of Re-ID as the same cow must be identified regardless of view. This point is also critical for BCS as some informative body parts might be invisible from some viewpoints. Viewpoint corresponds to the direction of the cow in relation to the camera. It is not the same as camera identity.

The experiment conducted by Grolleau et al.~\cite{grolleau2026moo} used synthetic cows created using fixed camera views. The advantage of such images is that, knowing the angles of view, one can analyze the effect of geometric variations independent of the changes which happen in reality. Moreover, Grolleau et al.~\cite{grolleau2026moo} tried to see if the features extracted by learning from synthetic examples can be transferred to real cattle.

Synthetic images are good for viewpoint annotation, while real images test how well the features extracted are robust against changes in the appearance and environment. The main idea behind this approach is that the model should be invariant to some kinds of changes and sensitive to others, depending on the task. In Re-ID, it is required that the representation be invariant to any viewpoint variations of the same cow. On the other hand, in Behavior recognition, the variation in posture is an important element and should affect the model. In BCS, variation in scale does not preclude the usage of informative body shape cues. This is the reason why the thesis uses task-conditioned structured information instead of using the same invariant representation in all tasks.

\subsection{Temporal Representations for Activity Recognition}

The modeling of the temporal structure aids in detecting changes in time. Different types of temporal structure also depend on the type of representation used. RGB sequences provide information on the appearance and posture of the cow whereas keypoint sequences provide information on the body structure. Video models can detect information on space and time simultaneously. However, the two models differ in terms of retaining information as well as computing and labeling processes.

SlowFast network explicitly separates the aspects of appearance and motion using two streams with different frame rates~\cite{feichtenhofer2019slowfast}, where the first stream concentrates on spatial information and the second captures faster changes. SlowFast network is also used as the benchmarking technique in the CVB application~\cite{zia2023cvb}, making connection between general video recognition technique and behavior of cattle. It provides a stronger motivation for temporal modeling approach other than simple assumption of more being better.

A temporal convolution network may also be used. In comparing convolutional and recurrent sequence models, Bai et al.~\cite{bai2018tcn} found that temporal convolutions have shown promise in several sequence tasks. These models leverage temporal properties without needing to include a recurrent hidden state at each step. In relation to cattle behavior, this provides justification for using a light temporal network in place of image features when many visualizations must be compared.

Another way to encode motion is to consider body landmark relations over time. ST-GCN by Yan et al.~\cite{yan2018stgcn} is a model that uses graphs to represent the skeleton of a moving human being with connections between nodes in space and in time. This approach is applicable to the problem of interest due to modeling relations between body parts rather than modeling coordinates separately. Still, its use in the case of cattle depends on having correct landmarks definition and accurate estimation of poses. Therefore, the pose sequence should be seen as a different representation category and not a substitution for RGB video.

The approaches prove that different temporal inputs address different problems. Taking the average of several frame features provides the model with more observable appearance, while a temporal model can also make use of the sequence of those frames. Pose features concentrate on body pose, while dense video contains more motion and contextual information. This choice is based on the type of action and sampling period. Hence, this thesis makes use of temporal modeling as a representation problem rather than scaling up the model.

\subsection{Shortcut Learning, Domain Shift, and Evaluation}

Good performance in a certain environment does not necessarily mean good performance in another environment. Geirhos et al. demonstrated how deep learning representations can base themselves on simple associations which may be vulnerable in new environments. The matter becomes even more critical in case the object and the background of interest recur often. For instance, images of cattle can always include the same pen, illumination, and perspective, which may make it easy for the algorithm to take advantage of some scene characteristics.

It is possible to test the dependence of the algorithm on the background directly. Xiao et al.~\cite{xiao2021background} vary the information about the foreground and background in order to find out their role in image identification. The results of their experiment show that the evaluation should not be restricted to using the same dataset only.

In case of cattle-oriented learning, the major point will be whether the algorithm continues paying attention to the cow under varying parts of the scene. Foreground processing and changes in camera, setting, or dataset can also be used to test this robustness. According to Beery et al.~\cite{beery2018terra}, there is an enormous discrepancy in terms of model performance between familiar camera-trap sites and unfamiliar camera-trap sites. Camera traps for wild animals are distinct from livestock cameras; however, they both may have the same fixed background repeated multiple times. The WILDS benchmark~\cite{koh2021wilds} also considers this problem using the benchmarks which feature natural distribution shift in terms of wildlife and other cases.

Thus, models need to be tested under various conditions, rather than considering all test images as challenging. The domain shift is not confined only to background change. The domain shift can include such factors as different cows, new breeds, new body appearances, new camera heights, as well as different label definitions. Re-ID studies~\cite{yu2025multicam,zhang2026beca} analyze some of these variations via multi-camera and long term settings. It is also important to take into account the image condition as well as whether the labels have the same meaning across different datasets.

It is also critical to split the evaluation data appropriately. As Kapoor and Narayanan~\cite{kapoor2023leakage} demonstrated, data leakage can lead to an erroneous estimation of performance for machine learning approaches. For video datasets, it is possible that two images might still refer to the same continuous sequence of events. Consequently, random splitting of images would mean that very similar scenarios would be included both in training and test sets. The correct grouping of the dataset would be a more effective approach in such cases. The choice of the correct grouping unit will depend on the type of evaluation. For unseen-cow evaluation, the correct cow ID is needed, and grouping by recording avoids another kind of overlapping.

In this sense, the design of the evaluation procedure is part of the representation problem. The mask will seem relevant in one split since the model is dealing with an easy identity and localization task. The use of multiple datasets will bring variation visually while one dataset is still linked to one class. Evaluation and training should be aligned with what is claimed in this case. This brings more validity to testing whether the use of cattle information helps with transfer rather than improving performance within one known data set.

\subsection{Multi-Task Learning: Selective Sharing and Negative Transfer}

The use of multitask learning (MTL) allows the combining of the three models into one system. The most basic design uses a single encoder and different heads for each task. This helps in saving computational resources and encourages the use of common features. However, the use of MTL requires more than the fact that there are three inputs containing cattle. Crawshaw~\cite{crawshaw2020mtl} notes that architecture, task relationships, and optimization all have roles in MTL performance. Thus, the sharing of the resources should have something more compelling than just the fact that the three inputs have cattle.

Some of the methods used in MTL attempt to ensure the balance of task updates. In Kendall et al.~\cite{kendall2018multi}, loss weighting is done using task uncertainty. The other technique is called GradNorm, which uses gradients for modifying the task weight depending on learning pace~\cite{chen2018gradnorm}. These techniques seek to prevent dominance of a particular task during joint learning due to either loss magnitude or pace. They control the strength of each task's update to the network. However, they do not select features to be shared visually.

PCGrad solves a completely different aspect of the problem through the modification of gradients during conflicting task updates~\cite{yu2020pcgrad}. Thus, gradient interaction becomes a component of the optimization process. Modification of the magnitude of an update and modification of a conflicting direction are two separate things, and therefore, PCGrad cannot be equated to loss weighting. Both these techniques remain within a selected architecture, thus allowing researchers to explore optimization as one of the causes of negative transfer.

Another approach alters feature sharing between tasks. Cross-Stitch Networks~\cite{misra2016crossstitch} learn how to share activations between task-specific networks. The Multi-Task Attention Network presented by Liu et al.~\cite{liu2019mtan} employs task-specific attention for selecting the relevant data from common features. It implies that all the tasks do not necessarily have to follow the same feature pathway. Such an approach can prove to be helpful if the tasks are somehow related but at the same time, require the extraction of different information, like cattle body structure, behavior, and identity.

Task clustering can be evaluated instead of being predetermined beforehand. Standley et al.~\cite{standley2020tasks} considered the question of the most suitable task clustering if the number of resources is limited. The research conducted by the authors showed that the relation between the tasks cannot be defined just based on the names or descriptions of the tasks. Two tasks may share early visual features but require different processing later, and one task grouping may help one task while making another worse. Good models for each single task need to be achieved before testing how good a joint model is.

Previous research works on animal vision have demonstrated both benefits and drawbacks of multitask learning. The SAAM-VetNet~\cite{attri2025saamvetnet} is designed for disease detection and classification of its severity via attention-based multitask learning. Also, CATR-Net~\cite{feng2025catr} joins cattle face segmentation and recognition. It is shown that multitask learning and perception-based recognition could be helpful in the case of animal vision. Nevertheless, the relationship between tasks in the previous studies differs significantly from the relationship in the present thesis. Segmentation and face recognition are oriented towards one visible object, whereas the disease type and its severity rely on similar local symptoms. Meanwhile, Body Condition Score, Behavior, and Re-ID require more diverse types of information.

Re-ID relies on the features which stay constant across the images of the same cow. Behavior recognition requires a response when the same cow changes its activities. Body Condition Score needs information about body shape without replacing condition information with cow-specific appearance. The common encoder would be possible for all three tasks in case it maintains the distinction between them. That is why the thesis contrasts hard sharing with task-conditioned or partially independent encoding.

The application of different datasets to the tasks poses another challenge. Joint training involves the perception of differences in perspective, composition, and style of annotation alongside the difference in tasks' goals. Consequently, some distinctions between tasks may be related to the used datasets. The fair comparison would involve maintenance of each task's evaluation protocol and analysis of results of every task separately. It would help to reveal whether the selective sharing really improves the model or only increases its size.

\section{Summary of Key Findings}

Therefore, the research problem is not limited by merging of three cattle classifiers. BCS, Behavior, and Re-ID need different types of visual information. BCS based on anatomy, Behavior models based on time, and Re-ID based on metric learning have different solutions for cattle monitoring problems. Each of them emphasizes different features of the cows.

\subsection{A Common Animal, but Different Information Requirements}

Thus, there should be a unified approach that considers the differences of all of them. BCS is based on the degree of body covering, body shape, and BCS score. Behavior Recognition depends on the movement, duration, and interactions with the environment. Re-ID depends on the consistent appearance of the cow in the various observation cases. This may be complementary in some cases, however, it may also lead to conflict situations. For example, the absence of individual textures can be helpful in avoiding identity-based BCS shortcuts, but it is required in case of Re-ID task. The presence of scene information may help in detecting feeding behavior, but it can create a shortcut for Re-ID.

Different approaches exist previously for different cattle monitoring tasks. Research gap \#1 is that it remains unclear how the above-mentioned options will impact BCS, Behavior, and Re-ID, as well as what will be the performance of cattle-centric representations against RGB representations under the consideration of all three tasks in one unified framework.

\subsection{Representation Quality Must Be Connected to Robust Evaluation}

Perception-based models can provide masks, landmarks, and viewpoint data; however, the generation of the output is not enough in terms of ensuring that the resulting representation contains all the necessary data for the considered tasks depending on changing conditions. In the case of BCS, these conditions involve cattle's body anatomy and conditions of capturing an image. In the case of Behavior, they involve cattle's posture and movements as well as changes in the scenery. And in the case of Re-ID, they involve different perspectives of one and the same cow in different locations or at different times.

This creates research gap \#2, where changes in representation need to be tested under evaluation conditions that match the expected type of change. Foreground-oriented models need to be assessed using cross-setting evaluations and class-wise results and not just an overall figure. Temporal models require to be evaluated using cross-temporal sampling of observations. Thus, this thesis investigates the relationship between visual data, task demands, and generalization settings.

\subsection{Selective Sharing as the Basis of the Unified Framework}

Multi-task learning studies consider two problems, that is the problem of balancing updates from different tasks and sharing information between different tasks. Early experiments in this thesis find that this distinction is significant in cattle monitoring applications. Rather than assuming that high feature sharing is desirable, first, we investigate the needs of each task individually using single-task models.

Then different multi-task learning schemes are considered and compared. The study seeks to make use of the similarity in the cattle but pays varying attention to body shape, behavior, and individuality. The main comparison will be between the generic RGB baselines and hard-sharing approaches. The study will focus more on task performance and negative transfer. Moreover, which features can be shared, which cannot be shared unless task-specific processing is done, and how they influence the integration will be investigated.

The literature review above will serve as a foundation for the subsequent chapters. Task constraints are provided in Chapter 3. Data protocols, representation, and model comparison will be discussed in Chapter 4. Chapter 5 will address the research questions using collected data.
