\section{Performance Evaluation}

The experiments will consist of all the results of the single task and multiple tasks experiments, which consist of Hard-Shared Baseline, Modular Task-Specific Model, and PCGrad control experiment.

\subsection{Body Condition Scoring Results}

The Baseline RGB Run 1 serves as the principal reference result for the ScienceDB images used for testing which number 8,040 in total. The physical MAE achieved by the model is equal to 0.1848 BCS units, while the Acc@1 value is 86.74\%. Most of the time, the prediction error was within $\pm0.25$ units, while predicting the score precisely was not easy. This should be considered as one estimate since the split was disjoint only among the burst groups and not cows.\evidence{E08}

The Perception-Enhanced model (Run~4) requires both valid bounding-box detection and non-empty segmentation. The complete detector--segmenter pipeline succeeded on 7,549 of 8,040 test images, achieving 93.89\% perception coverage (489 detection misses and two empty masks). To prevent sample attrition from confounding comparisons, the baseline model was re-evaluated on this identical 7,549-image matched subset.\evidence{E24}

\input{tables/bcs_comparison}

The single-task experiment will begin by investigating whether the cattle-specific representation is superior to the existing RGB representation. On the other hand, the multiple task experiments will address the question of joint learning and ask whether negative transfer happens when the only feature representation is the same spatial representation. The multiple task experiments will also examine whether the use of multiple task-specific models will compensate for the loss in performance, as well as whether PCGrad resolves the gradient conflict in the shared backbone network.

In the matched subset, the model with improved perception showed better results in primary ordinal metrics (table of summary), by decreasing the physical MAE from 0.1929 to 0.1709 BCS units and tolerance accuracy (Acc@1) from 84.95\% to 89.40\%. In contrast to this, there were no improvements for class-balanced metrics: the metrics such as balanced accuracy and Macro-F1 remained unchanged or even slightly worsened. Thus, cow-centered inputs have narrowed the gap between predicted and real intervals of scores but have not positively impacted class-balanced metrics.

\subsection{Behavior Recognition Results}

Our findings justify the use of localized crops and masks for assisting image understanding. It is impossible to determine the contribution of segmentation with a limited spatial scale since all three novel approaches have been implemented simultaneously. Additionally, our results do not include 6.11\% of test cases where automatic perception did not work.

\subsection{Cattle Re-Identification Results}

The open set biometric identification experiment was done by using SideView Protocol~A. The system scanned through a fixed database of 36,811 images from the parlor using barn CCTV and handheld camera images. The experiment involved 69 cows that were not among the 41 training cows.\evidence{E22,E29}

\input{tables/reid_barn_results}

In case of Barn-to-Parlor experiments, the Oracle Segmentation-Guided model (Run~6) enhanced the main retrieval performance in Table~\ref{tab:reidbarn}. Accuracy for Rank-1 increased from 58.64\% to 63.90\%, whereas the mAP increased from 38.32\% to 40.68\%. It indicates that the model performed better when identifying the right cow among the retrieved images. But the performance on the deeper ranks was more or less similar or even worse.

\input{tables/reid_snapshot_results}

The handheld Snapshot-to-Parlor evaluation yielded a substantially larger effect (Table~\ref{tab:reidsnapshot}). Under unconstrained mobile queries, Rank-1 accuracy rose from 38.88\% to 62.93\% (+24.05 pp), alongside an mAP rise from 27.05\% to 40.42\%. The oracle target-centered configuration produced its largest improvement under the Snapshot-to-Parlor setting, where acquisition differences were greater.

These results need to be considered in the context of oracle evaluation. Given that the ground truth masks have been used in Run 6 for cropping and channel concatenation, these results cannot confirm that the automation of the upstream segmentation task would yield the same improvements in practice. \section{Multi-Task Learning Results}

In order to assess the performance of all three tasks jointly, all models using multiple tasks were validated using the same matching held-out datasets as the single task models. For Body Condition Scoring, 7,549 ScienceDB images were employed, using burst-group-disjoint split, where identical passages were kept separate, but it was not a biological cow-disjoint split. For Behavior Recognition, 780 sequence samples were employed, each having 8 frames. In total, 6,240 frames were used for evaluation, consisting of 422 surveillance sequences from CVB, 358 feedlot sequences from Kaggle Beef dataset, while the Walking class was only present in the CVB dataset. For Cattle Re-Identification, SideView Protocol A was employed using 69 unseen cattle and a gallery containing 36,811 parlor images, together with 25,260 barn queries and 607 snapshot queries, using the provided oracle target masks. Only the best checkpoint was determined based on the lowest combined validation loss. The test sets were never seen during training and model selection and only evaluated once after training was done.

\subsection{Body Condition Scoring Multi-Task Outcomes}

The Table~\ref{tab:mtl_bcs_results} demonstrates that BCS performance deteriorated when hard-shared multi-task approach (E1) was applied relative to the performance of the baseline (Run~4). Physical mean absolute error became equal to 0.1709 for Run~4 and 0.1788 for E1. Other metrics like tolerance accuracy and balanced performance metric became worse as well. Ordinal cross-entropy loss became somewhat better but did not contribute to the improvement of BCS score prediction.

\input{tables/mtl_bcs_results}

Neither modular adapters nor gradient projection overcame this performance deficit. Introducing task-private bottleneck adapters (E3) degraded performance further, resulting in the highest error across all models ($\mathrm{MAE} = 0.1916$). The PCGrad Hard-Shared Optimization Control (E4) partially mitigated this loss, lowering MAE to 0.1828 and recovering tolerance accuracy relative to E3. However, E4 still trailed the dedicated single-task reference ($\mathrm{MAE} = 0.1709$). Crucially, all three multi-task configurations underperformed the single-task model on primary physical and balanced metrics, confirming persistent negative transfer for body condition scoring under joint training.

\subsection{Behavior Recognition Multi-Task Outcomes}

Joint training under naive hard sharing (E1) produced severe degradation across balanced evaluation measures (Table~\ref{tab:mtl_behavior_results}). Balanced accuracy plunged from 74.43\% in the single-task reference to 67.30\%, accompanied by sharp declines in overall accuracy and Macro-F1. The minority Walking class was disproportionately impacted, decreasing from an F1 of 0.2456 to 0.0408. Modular adapters (E3) offered only selective, loss-specific relief; while E3 improved test loss and overall accuracy, balanced accuracy eroded further (66.70\%) and Walking F1 fell to 0.0000.

\input{tables/mtl_behavior_results}

The PCGrad optimization control (E4) was the most effective multi-task variant for Behavior, partially mitigating hard-sharing degradation. E4 raised balanced accuracy to 69.15\% and recovered Walking F1 to 0.0909 (more than doubling E1). Moreover, on CVB surveillance (Table~\ref{tab:mtl_behavior_source}), E4 reached an overall accuracy of 81.04\%, marginally surpassing the single-task reference. However, source-disjoint results emphasize that overall accuracy and class-balanced fidelity diverge: CVB balanced accuracy (55.30\%) and Walking F1 under E4 remained far below the dedicated single-task reference (61.05\% and 0.2456, respectively). Thus, while PCGrad provided clear optimization-level mitigation, it did not fully restore single-task discriminative power on difficult minority states.

\subsection{Cattle Re-Identification Multi-Task Outcomes}

The dedicated single-task oracle model (Run~6) maintained the strongest performance across both query settings, achieving a Rank-1 accuracy of 63.90\% on Barn queries and 62.93\% on Snapshot queries, with mAP exceeding 40\% in both environments (Table~\ref{tab:mtl_reid_results}). Monolithic hard sharing (E1) degraded these principal retrieval measures considerably, dropping Barn Rank-1 to 57.38\% and Snapshot Rank-1 to 57.17\%, alongside substantial losses in ranking precision ($\mathrm{mAP}$).

\input{tables/mtl_reid_results}

Different results were obtained from the architecture and optimization approaches in Re-ID. The modular adapters (E3) approach decreased the performance to Barn Rank-1 of 49.08\%. PCGrad (E4) improved the performance in comparison with the previous one, and achieved a result of Barn Rank-1 of 54.97\%, and a higher value of Snapshot mAP of 33.87\%. Nevertheless, its results were inconsistent with E1 and were still lower than those of the single-task network. Minor improvements occurred at higher ranks, for example, Snapshot Rank-10 was slightly increased in E3. It can be concluded that there is a difference in the retrieval performance by the implementation of multiple tasks for feature sharing; however, this method is still not as accurate as a specific single-task Re-ID approach.

\subsection{Observed Shared-Backbone Gradient Conflict}

Table~\ref{tab:pcgrad_diagnostics} reports the gradient conflict diagnostics recorded in E4 PCGrad training across all the 30 epochs, which consist of 16,140 super-steps. Overall, 44,177 pairwise conflict projections have been noted during training. The average number of conflict projections per super-step was 2.737, while the highest possible number was 6. There were also similarities among the conflict rates of the different task pairs. Conflicts between BCS and Behavior occurred in 47.8\% of super-steps, BCS and Re-ID in 46.5\%, and Behavior and Re-ID in 47.4\%.

\input{tables/pcgrad_gradient_diagnostics}

As per the training diagnostics, conflicting gradients are observed quite often in the four-channel ResNet-18 backbone that is shared by both tasks. Nevertheless, one has to make sure that an essential distinction between E1 and E4 is not forgotten. E1 just summed up the gradients, whereas E4 applied the PCGrad method in order to determine the conflicting gradient directions and to project them. As a result, the E4 results demonstrate that conflicts are often observed only in the case of E4 training. They cannot be used in arguing that E1 experienced an equivalent rate of conflict or that gradient interference is the only explanation for the performance difference.

\section{Analysis of Multi-Task Design Solutions}

The empirical evaluations across single-task and multi-task configurations support distinct findings regarding the three multi-task design hypotheses:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item \textbf{Monolithic Hard Parameter Sharing (E1)}: For E1, all three tasks share the same ResNet-18 spatial backbone. In doing so, worse results were obtained for BCS, Behavior, and Re-ID. These findings demonstrate negative transfer since the performance of all three tasks does not necessarily improve when they are learned together. It might be due to the fact that the information used by these three tasks is different: BCS relies on the body structure, Behavior on the movement and Re-ID on minute visual details. On the other hand, E1 cannot identify exactly which shared features were impacted and how they were impacted.
 \item \textbf{Modular Task-Private Adapters (E3)}: Adapter blocks were incorporated into E3 for each task between the shared backbone and the prediction head. Nonetheless, they did not solve consistently the problem of negative transfer. Some surveillance-related metrics and losses became better, while BCS and Re-ID performances declined, *along with Behavior balanced accuracy*. In addition to that, E3 contributed 395,904 parameters for training, thus enlarging the model capacity by 3.32\%. As a result, any enhancement in performance cannot be attributed solely to task-specific routing.
 \item \textbf{PCGrad Optimization Control (E4)}: E4 modified the optimization strategy while maintaining the same model capacity. It had the same amount of trainable parameters as E1 - 11,926,706 - and didn't incorporate any adapter layers. PCGrad contributed to a reduction of negative transfer in several areas, such as Behavior accuracy, balanced accuracy, Macro-F1 score, and *CVB surveillance performance*. Moreover, it outperformed E3 in terms of important BCS and Re-ID metrics. Nevertheless, E4 performed worse than single-task models in some significant metrics.
\end{enumerate}

Table~\ref{tab:decisions} synthesizes the key design decisions and empirical dispositions established across the single-task and multi-task investigations.

\input{tables/design_decisions}

The findings indicate that neither simple task-specific adapters nor gradient projection alone was sufficient to alleviate negative transfer effects in all three of the cattle monitoring tasks. The individual models were better in several task-specific measures. This corroborates the primary conclusion of the thesis that different tasks require different kinds of representations, and their combination in multi-task models may yield *different trade-offs depending on the metric*.

\section{Statistical Analysis and Uncertainty Boundaries}

All single-task and multi-task models in this chapter were evaluated based on only one trained checkpoint, and the reported results are point estimates. Evaluation with repeated training using different seeds, confidence intervals, and hypothesis testing was not done. Thus, the differences described above are purely descriptive comparisons that cannot be deemed statistically significant. The matched-sample design still yields fair internal comparisons since the same test data sets are used: 7,549 images of ScienceDB for BCS, 780 sequences for Behavior, and SideView Protocol A for Re-ID.

Nevertheless, such a comparison does not take into account variations of the different training seeds or the entire population variation. Furthermore, related video frames or image bursts are not supposed to be regarded as separate biological samples.

Similarly, the PCGrad conflict rates provided in the Table~\ref{tab:pcgrad_diagnostics} represent only one observation during the training process. Minor differences between metrics, like the 0.18 percentage point difference in snapshot mAP between E4 and E1, cannot be considered equivalent.

\section{Cross-Task Relationships and Claim Boundaries}

The three evaluation domains employ incommensurable metrics with distinct scales and optimization objectives:
\begin{itemize}
 \item \textbf{BCS}: For the case of BCS, the metrics consist of physical Mean Absolute Error in BCS units (the smaller is better), Acc@1 within $\pm$0.25 units, and ordinal accuracy.
 \item \textbf{Behavior}: For the Behavior task, the metrics comprise accuracy, balanced accuracy, Macro-F1 score across five classes, and F1-score for each of the five classes (the larger is better).
 \item \textbf{Re-ID}: For Re-ID, the metrics employed in the evaluation are Rank-1, Rank-5, Rank-10, and mean Average Precision for open-set ranking, where the higher the number, the better.
\end{itemize}
As these measures cannot be adequately aggregated to give a combined score, negative transfer must be assessed for each task individually using comparison with its corresponding single-task model.

The evaluation cohorts also define strict generalization boundaries:
\begin{itemize}
 \item \textbf{ScienceDB}: ScienceDB evaluates novel burst groups formed from automatic passages but does not validate them as novel biological cows.
 \item \textbf{Behavior}: Behavior evaluates protected video and sessions but not cow-disjoint herds. The Walking task is evaluated only on CVB surveillance data.
 \item \textbf{SideView Protocol A}: In the SideView Protocol A, the test dataset comprises 69 novel biological cows, which were not seen during the representation learning phase. However, the Re-ID models use the released ground-truth masks. It implies that the test results were gathered using oracle segmentation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion of Findings}

The empirical findings presented in this chapter synthesize into three overarching insights:

The external data sets listed in the broader research project plan, including Ruchay, Dryad, MmCows, CBVD-5, and BECA, have yet to be validated and will undergo stress testing in the future. It means that just mentioning these data sets is not enough to imply that the model has learned to apply the knowledge gained to other data sets or environments.

In single-task experiments, cattle-centered visual data were beneficial against regular RGB data. Nonetheless, the amount of benefit was varied depending on the task. Localized cropping and binary mask guidance helped with BCS by improving the closeness of the estimated physical score. They also helped with Re-ID by improving open set retrieval with challenging handheld camera viewpoints. Regarding Behavior, temporal foreground modeling enhanced class-balanced discrimination and detection of minority classes while redistributing accuracy among source distributions (Tablestab:behaviorcomparison-tab:behaviorsourcecomparison). This shows the benefit of extracting animal foregrounds, even while making clear that the use of foreground information is not an absolute cure-all for each sub-metric.

Second, hard parameter sharing resulted in consistent negative transfer effects at the level of performance across all three modalities. With respect to the respective single-task baselines, shared backbone learning for the three tasks increased BCS physical error (summary table), reduced Behavior balanced accuracy and minority class detection of walking behavior (summary table), and decreased Re-ID retrieval accuracy (summary table). Such consistent negative transfer effects do not indicate automatic benefit from hard sharing in these different tasks. Third, neither increased model capacity nor gradient projection addressed this task interference effect.

Despite the use of separate residual bottleneck adapters for each task in E3, while the results of Behavior surveillance got slightly better, those of BCS and Re-ID were even worse. On the other hand, the gradient projection method applied in E4 provided an even better, yet much more selective improvement. It yielded the best results for Behavior among the multi-task models and managed to recover the performance lost in BCS ordinal scoring and Re-ID retrieval, while using the exact same amount of parameters as the hard-shared baseline. Moreover, the results from training presented in the summary table reveal that there were conflicts between the gradients of the tasks rather often during their joint training on the shared backbone. Nevertheless, the separate single-task models performed even better than the multi-task models with regard to all main metrics of the three tasks. Thus, the results did not reveal the usefulness of simultaneous learning of all tasks. In addition, the results revealed that different tasks require different kinds of representation and optimization, which cannot be provided by a shared backbone. That is why multi-task agricultural monitoring requires thorough metric comparison.
